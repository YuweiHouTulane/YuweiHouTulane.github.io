Research

I work on why bone is lost with age, and on what the cells and genes involved are actually doing. That means moving between scales — genome-wide association data at one end, single cells and their spatial arrangement in tissue at the other, and, from an earlier life in epidemiology, whole populations.

Current projects

  • Mapping the signaling between bone and muscle

    Bone and skeletal muscle sit against each other and communicate constantly — through cytokines, growth factors and extracellular matrix proteins — but little was known about where in the tissue that conversation actually happens. We applied 10x Visium spatial transcriptomics to mouse femur and the muscle beside it, resolved eight major cell types, and inferred the ligand–receptor pairs carrying signal across the boundary. Collagen, tenascin and thrombospondin pathways all converge on Sdc4; immunostaining confirmed those pairs sit where the model put them, and independent mouse and human single-cell datasets reproduced most of the result.

    10x Visium · CellChat · Bone Research, 2026

  • Genetics of osteoporosis and skeletal aging

    Association studies have turned up a great many loci for bone mineral density, but a variant is not a mechanism. This strand of my work runs genome-wide association analysis on skeletal traits and tries to connect the signals back to the cell types and expression programs that could plausibly carry them — which is where the single-cell and spatial work earns its keep.

    GWAS · Single-cell RNA-seq · Multi-omics integration

  • Why chronic disease risk varies by geography

    The “diabetes belt” is a band of US counties where diabetes prevalence runs well above the national rate. Using responses from about 398,000 adults in the 2019 BRFSS, we modeled that gap with logistic regression and random forests to rank what explains it. Low socioeconomic status, physical inactivity and hypertension came out on top — which says something concrete about where intervention would do the most good.
