Research

I work on why bone is lost with age, and on what the cells and genes involved are actually doing. That means moving between scales — genome-wide association data at one end, single cells and their spatial arrangement in tissue at the other, and, from an earlier life in epidemiology, whole populations.

Current projects

  • A spatial map of the bone niche, linked to osteoporosis genetics

    Single-cell atlases tell us which cells live in bone, but breaking the tissue apart loses the layout that defines a niche — and with it any way to say where genetic risk for osteoporosis plays out. We profiled a mouse femoral head with 10x Visium HD spatial transcriptomics, resolving skeletal and muscle cells across the trabecular marrow, endosteal surface, articular cartilage, periosteum and myotendinous junction. Cell–cell communication analysis suggests signaling runs both ways between bone and muscle. Integrating bone-density GWAS with gsMap concentrates genetic risk at the periosteum and myotendinous junction, where Sparc and Fgfr1 emerge as candidate genes.
